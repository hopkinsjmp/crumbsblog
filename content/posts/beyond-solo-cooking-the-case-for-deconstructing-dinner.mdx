I’m fully prepared for people to scoff at what I’m about to write. Some might think: ‘why is this even important?’ or, ‘you actually wrote about this?’. And then there’ll be those who’ll claim that the following philosophy is starkly incompatible with a busy lifestyle or life’s other, more important, priorities. 

I get it, but you’re reading this now so hear me out. As the title suggests, I’m a Deconstructionist when it comes to preparing meals for more than myself. It all came from the usual causes of any cook’s stress: struggling with time, not thinking (and therefore shopping) ahead, having a frustrating lack of ideas, needing to be budget-conscious, or—on the rare but exciting days—having a hankering for a very specific ingredient that didn’t really make ‘sense’ as a dinner component or a standalone dish.

So—by accident more than anything else—I got in the habit of, instead of trying to think up a cohesive ‘meal’, rustling up a combination of dishes that worked in the moment. Keeping dinner disconnected in this sense, means that: 

- I don’t need to have the prior wherewithal to figure out what I want to cook (say, burgers) and with that, the additions that ‘complete’ it (buns, salad and the like).
- I can make use of leftovers that I have otherwise no idea what to do with (usually spinach leaves—there’s always more in the bag than you think there is). 
- I can include those culprits I have a hankering for (embarrassingly often, a mozzarella or burrata swimming in olive oil).
- I can keep my cooking medium options open (not everything has to be hot or cooked).
- If something goes ‘wrong’ during the cooking process, then I haven’t ‘ruined’ the entire meal. I just leave it out (or, because I’m lazy, still serve it with a generous helping of apologies).

And when it comes to plating up, I just lay out the ‘dishes’—either in the kitchen or on the dining table—allowing anyone/everyone to pick and eat as much as they want (I find this helps with portion control; I feel fuller quicker if I’m grazing). Plus, this supremely lazy attitude to cooking tends to look anything but that. 

Depending on how you cook—i.e. whether you tend to have leftovers in the fridge or a relatively well-stocked pantry—this strategy may or may not be for those who can’t multitask (but such is all cooking). However, I would argue that the Deconstructionist approach is typically less difficult and time-consuming than making one unified meal in one sitting. Yes, it is disjointed cooking in a sense but as mentioned, has the benefits of on-the-fly planning and doesn’t discount easy dishes that wouldn’t normally be in the ‘dinner’ repertoire: think cooking up eggs in any which way, rustling up instant miso soup, steaming any vegetables handy—subsequently sprinkling with salt and olive oil, or perhaps grilling a steak to then slice and serve as is, offering also a tangle of seasoned courgetti spaghetti that can be spiralised in seconds (and in my opinion, go with everything). 

And the best part, if I may add? This form of dining always, always keeps the possibility of a makeshift cheeseboard (however modest) on the cards. And who, let’s be honest, is not elated at the idea of that?
